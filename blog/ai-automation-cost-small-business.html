What AI Automation Actually Costs a Small Business in 2026

The software is usually the cheap part. Here is where the money goes, with dated prices, and how to keep a first project small enough to pay for itself.

By PJ Sojdei, founder of Codegrity. Published October 19, 2026. 7 minute read.

The short answer

For one well-chosen workflow, most small businesses should expect to spend somewhere in the low thousands of dollars to have it built, and tens to a few hundred dollars a month to run it. Published 2026 ranges line up with that. One independent consultant puts a defined small-business automation project at roughly $2,000 to $15,000 (Bushe, May 2026). A development agency's 2026 budget guide puts a single workflow at $7,000 to $12,000 and a multi-workflow setup at $15,000 to $25,000 (KrishaWeb, July 2026).

The spread is wide because "AI automation" covers everything from a single email-sorting step to a system that touches every part of the business. The useful question isn't "what does AI cost", it's "what does this one piece of work cost us today, and what would it cost to hand it to software".

The four costs

Every automation project has the same four line items. Most surprises come from the last two.

CostTypical range
Deciding what to automate (an audit)$0 to a few thousand
Building the first workflowLow thousands to $15,000
Running it: tools and AI usageTens to hundreds a month
Keeping it workingA few hours a month

1. Deciding what to automate

You can do this yourself for free, and for a simple case you should. Paid assessments range from fixed-fee diagnostics of a few hundred dollars to audits of several thousand: one firm sells a $297 five-day diagnostic, another prices its audit at $3,500 to $8,500 over two to four weeks (Petronella Tech, Main & Machine). What you are paying for is someone experienced telling you which work is worth automating and which isn't, before you spend on a build.

2. Building it

This is engineering time: connecting your systems, writing the steps, testing on real data and handling the cases where something goes wrong. It is the biggest single cost, and it scales with how many systems are involved and how clean their data is, not with how impressive the AI part sounds.

3. Running it

This is where most people overestimate. Workflow tools are inexpensive at small-business volumes: Zapier's Professional plan was $19.99 a month billed annually and its Team plan $69, as of June 2026 (eesel). Plenty of automations run on scheduled jobs in services you already pay for and add nothing at all.

AI model usage is billed per token, roughly per word. As of mid-October 2026, small, fast models from the major providers cost around $0.10 to $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.50 to $0.60 per million output tokens, while the largest frontier models cost about $10 in and $50 out (GEO Toolbox, checked October 17, 2026).

A worked example. Say an automation reads 2,000 customer emails a month, about 1,500 tokens each, and writes a 300-token summary or draft reply for each one. That is 3 million tokens in and 600,000 out.

On a small model that is roughly $0.45 plus $0.36: under a dollar a month. On a frontier model it is about $30 plus $30, or $60 a month. Both are small next to an hour of someone's time, but the difference shows why we pick the model per step instead of using the biggest one everywhere.

4. Keeping it working

Automations break when the tools around them change: a vendor updates an API, a form gets a new field, a login expires. Budget a few hours a month for upkeep, and insist that whatever you build logs every run and alerts someone on failure. A silent failure is the expensive kind.

Where budgets blow up

  • Messy data. If the same customer exists three different ways in three systems, cleanup comes before automation. The KrishaWeb guide puts data preparation for multi-system consolidation at $5,000 to $25,000 on its own.
  • Automating everything at once. Each workflow added to the first project multiplies testing and the number of things that can go wrong.
  • Systems with no API. Older software that only exports files, or has no export at all, takes more work to connect reliably.
  • No owner. An automation nobody watches will fail quietly. Someone has to own it.
  • Regulated data. Health, financial and personal data add review, access controls and sometimes legal input.

How to keep the first project small and worth it

  1. Pick one workflow that happens often, follows rules, and someone already does by hand every week.
  2. Measure it first. Write down how many hours it takes today. Without a baseline you can't tell whether the automation paid off.
  3. Keep a person approving anything customer-facing or financial. Let the software do the preparation.
  4. Insist on logs and alerts from day one.
  5. Choose the cheapest tool that works for each step, including no AI at all where a plain rule does the job.
